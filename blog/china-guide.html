A short China travel guide

The apps and setup I'd sort before landing — connectivity, payments, maps, trains and food.

Navigating China as a traveller can feel daunting: the Great Firewall, a near-cashless economy, and a language barrier. Having the right setup on your phone before you land is the difference between a stressful first day and a smooth trip.

Payments & money

China is almost entirely cashless, and physical foreign credit cards are rarely accepted at local shops or street vendors.

  • Alipay (支付宝) — the number one payment app for international travellers. Link an international Visa or Mastercard; registering your passport identity in advance avoids security flags on your first purchases.
  • Revolut — connecting a Revolut card to Alipay worked like a charm for me. It is a handy card to carry for the trip anyway; sign up with my referral link (code georgiwq4).
  • Alipay Transport Code (支付宝乘车码) — built into Alipay. Generates a QR pass to scan at subway turnstiles and bus gates in major cities such as Shanghai, Beijing and Hangzhou.
  • Carry a little cash anyway — small vendors and emergencies still take RMB.

Navigation & local transport

  • Amap (高德地图) — China's premier navigation app, with real-time walking, driving and public-transit directions far better than Western map apps. Google Maps does not work reliably in mainland China.
  • MetroMan — clean, offline subway maps and route planners in English for almost every Chinese city with a metro.
  • Railway 12306 (中国铁路) — the official China Railway app: schedules and high-speed rail tickets with no added booking fees. Your passport is your ticket at the gates.
  • DiDi (滴滴) — ride-hailing with an English interface; show the driver the destination in Chinese characters.

Daily life, shopping & food

  • Meituan (美团) — food delivery to your hotel, restaurant discounts, group-buy meal vouchers, and even pharmacy orders.
  • Taobao (淘宝) — China's mega e-commerce platform, handy for bargain shopping or ordering items to your accommodation on longer stays.

My visit in Shanghai

If you have an afternoon in Shanghai, I'd suggest Shanghai Tower in Lujiazui. It is one of the tallest buildings in the world, and its "Top of Shanghai" observation deck on the 118th floor (around 546 m) puts the whole city — the Bund, the Huangpu River and the sprawl beyond — in a single view. Go up an hour or so before sunset so you see the city in daylight and again once the lights come on, and book a timed ticket in advance.

Pair it with a walk along the Bund or the ferry across the Huangpu — both are cheap, and both give you the tower from the outside.

Also newly opened and worth a look: the Shanghai Grand Opera House in the Expo Culture Park on the Pudong side. The Snøhetta-designed "Chinese Fan" has a spiralling roof you can climb for a public rooftop view, and the surrounding park is a pleasant half-day on its own. Take Metro Line 7 to Houtan and walk over; check ahead for free tour reservations and performance times.

Quick tips

  1. Download VPNs early. You cannot download or update VPN apps once you are behind the Great Firewall.
  2. Verify payments beforehand. Register your identity (passport) and link your cards on Alipay before departure to prevent security flags during your first purchases.
  3. Keep offline translation handy. Download offline Chinese packs just in case you lose signal in remote areas or on the subway.
  4. Carry your passport. It is always needed — hotels register your stay with it, it is your ticket at train gates, and you need it to buy a local SIM.
  5. Avoid morning rush hour. Roughly 7:30–9:30 is hard, especially around Hongqiao, where the railway station and airport form one huge transport hub — metro queues and security lines are long, so add 45–60 minutes to your plans.
